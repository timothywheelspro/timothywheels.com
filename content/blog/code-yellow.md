**One of the most secure networks in the world went dark on a routine fix. What saved the day wasn't the patch. It was the plan someone wrote before anyone needed it.**

When I started studying for Security+, the first model the material hands you is three letters: C, I, A. Confidentiality, integrity, availability.

Most people hear "security" and only think of the first one. Keep the wrong people out. Lock it down. Encrypt it. Firewall it.

The third letter gets less attention, but it's the one you feel. Availability: is the thing there when you need it?

On Tuesday, 17 November, the National Security Agency's classified internal network, NSANet, went offline. It's the system the agency's own people use to share classified information with each other.

Before anyone reaches for the movie version, here's what was reported that day: no evidence of a cyberattack, and a source telling the *Wall Street Journal* that the agency was updating its software to patch the system. A former senior intelligence official told the paper an attack was the least likely explanation.

That's not a breach story. That's a maintenance story. And maintenance stories are the ones worth studying, because they're the kind that happen to everyone.

It also wasn't the only one that week. The Friday before, a router failure disrupted air traffic control centers across the country. Since Friday the 13th we've seen more than one major system go down, and the stretch goes back at least as far as Covid. You don't have to believe anything dramatic to notice it. Systems fail. Patches break things. Vendors have bad days.

Now look at what the agency did next.

The phone line employees call for status said it was a "code yellow" day, the middle level of a three-level system. Staff who weren't essential were told to arrive in staggered shifts, no earlier than noon. Emergency staff reported as scheduled.

Nobody invented that this morning. The levels existed. The list of who counts as essential existed. The instruction was recorded and waiting. The fix might take hours. Deciding who shows up took seconds.

The plan ran before the fix did.

**Secure isn't the same as available**

This network sits behind about as much protection as a network can have. It still went dark, from the inside, during a routine update. The walls held. The lights still went out.

Most of us build our lives the same way. We protect our things: passwords, locks, insurance. We rarely ask what happens when the thing itself just stops. The phone that won't turn on. The bank app that's down on rent day. The car in the shop. The one paycheck that comes late.

Being protected and being available are different problems, and they need different answers.

**Every network has a single point of failure until you name it**

In networking, a single point of failure is any one part that takes everything down with it when it breaks. You don't find them by staring at the diagram. You find them by asking, part by part, "what if this is gone?"

Your life is a network too. For most people, a short list carries everything: one phone, one income, one vehicle, one account the money runs through, one person who can watch the kids.

None of that is a flaw. It's just unexamined. A single point of failure you've named is a risk. One you haven't is a surprise.

**Tier it before you need it**

The agency's three levels do something simple and powerful: they turn a crisis into a lookup. Nobody debates at 7 a.m. who should come in. They check the level and follow the line that's already written.

You can do the same thing at a much smaller scale. Not a binder. Three lines.

- **What still has to happen,** no matter what.
- **What can wait** until the system is back.
- **What the fallback is:** the other route, the backup, the person you call.

A plan like that isn't pessimism. It's what lets you stay calm when everyone else is improvising.

We've all been broke before, but we don't want to be broken. The difference is usually whether the plan existed before the bad day did.

**The exercise: write one code yellow**

Pick **one** system you depend on every day. Just one. Then answer three questions, in writing, in under five minutes:

1. **If it's down at 8 a.m. tomorrow, what still has to happen?** (Rent still gets paid. The kids still get picked up. The shift still gets worked.)
2. **What can wait?** Be honest. More can wait than it feels like in the moment.
3. **What's the fallback?** A second way to pay, a ride you can call, cash in an envelope, a number written on paper instead of saved only in the phone.

Put it somewhere you'll find it when the thing it's about is down. That part matters: a backup plan stored only on your phone doesn't help on the day the phone is the problem.

Three lines. Boring on purpose.

Next week, do one more system.

**The reframe**

You won't stop every outage. Nobody can, not you and not the most secure agency in the country. Patches break things. Vendors have bad days. Weather and timing don't ask.

Control was never the uptime. Uptime belongs to patches, vendors and timing nobody picks. Control is the plan you wrote before you needed it: who shows up, what waits, and where you go when the main road is closed.

Write it on a calm day. That's the only day it's easy.

This is how you Control Your World.
