# AiA House Style Skill

## Goal
Write or revise public-facing content in the Awareness In Action / Control Your World voice: an operator writing to operators, where a system metaphor (routing, control, layers, load, source/sink) carries the actual argument, and the payoff is always a move the reader can make. Accurate, never alarmist. Peer, never preachy.

## How to use this skill
This voice is learned by pattern, not rule. Before drafting, read all three brief/essay pairs at the bottom of this file:

- **Manifesto** — structure: three internal traits (communication, confidence, consistency).
- **Battery** — structure: three external cables (attention, labor, data), on a Power-over-Ethernet source/load metaphor. UNPUBLISHED reference draft (see the note on the examples).
- **Road** — structure: NOT "three of something" at all — a set of defensive-driving principles, with one (road rage) breaking out into its own section. A follow-up essay that opens by referencing the prior one.

These three have DELIBERATELY DIFFERENT skeletons. Borrow the MOVES, never the skeleton of any single one. Do not assume every essay is three-part. Do not reuse any one essay's specific metaphor, refrain, or closing sentence. Let the topic dictate the structure.

The examples live inline in this file on purpose. An earlier version kept them in an `examples/` directory, which failed to sync twice and left drafts built from the rules alone. Keep this skill one file.

First decide the format (essay or short post) and the voice (Fly in first person, or Contruil as "we"). Then follow the matching section below.

## The essay skeleton (flexible, not a template)
1. Hook subtitle: one line naming the system and the counter to it.
2. Open in first person, mid-realization — a real personal observation. Not a thesis, not a greeting, not a fill-in-the-blank.
3. Name the system and how it operates on the reader. Weave routing/control language in naturally.
4. The turn: pivot into the central insight. Default to stating the insight directly. When a spoken pivot fits, use one of the author's own phrasings (see PIVOTS under Hard constraints).
5. Layered breakdown: 2-4 named sections, each a bold header. For each: name it, show how the system uses it, give the counter-move, end on a short standalone line. Every layer must load-bear — say what moves through it and what breaks without it. Never relabel nouns and call it a stack.
6. The exercise: one concrete, small, deliberately boring rep per concept.
7. The reframe: restate the thesis as something the reader owns.
8. Sign off with the series closer: "This is how you Control Your World."
9. References & context (real sources), then a short third-person bio.

## Short-format posts (LinkedIn feed, company announcements)
Use this for feed posts, launch announcements, and anything under roughly 300 words. The moves stay; the essay scaffolding comes off.

- **Length:** about 150–300 words.
- **Voice:** Fly's personal posts open in first person ("I"). Contruil company posts — product launches, milestones, tools — use "we." Pick one and hold it; never mix "I" and "we" in the same post.
- **Keep:** a ripened opener (not reportage of today), the system named before the turn, 2-3 bold mini-layers, one rep for the reader, the "Control was never the X" reframe, and the closer "This is how you Control Your World."
- **Drop:** the hook subtitle, references section, and bio. None of them are required in a post.
- **Links:** never in the post body. Close the post with a line pointing to the first comment, and draft that first comment alongside the post.
- **Product facts:** name tools by their real on-site names and describe only features that are confirmed live. If you cannot confirm a current detail (name, features, scoring, model lineup), use a bracketed placeholder like [CONFIRM CURRENT FEATURES] instead of guessing.

## Two timing rules the examples demonstrate
- **The opener is an observation that ripened, not a thing that just happened.** "A while back I started paying attention to what was paying attention to me." "One night, tired in that specific way where you've done everything and somehow gained nothing, I did something I'd never thought to do." Never open as reportage of today's events.
- **The realization arrives after a full section of evidence, not after one paragraph.** In all three examples the system is named, illustrated, and felt before the pivot fires. Firing it early wastes it. In short posts, "a full section" shrinks to a few lines, but the order holds: evidence first, turn second.

## Signature moves (vary the words, keep the move)
- Let the evidence lead into the insight, or use one of the author's pivot phrasings (see PIVOTS). Vary the words with the piece.
- "That's not [easy negative read]. That's [truer reframe]." — VARY it every time ("That's not despair. That's just the road."). Never harden into one pair.
- Short standalone lines for rhythm.
- A refrain that recurs once or twice WITHIN a piece — but invent a fresh one per essay. Do not import another essay's refrain (e.g. do not graft "Be about your word, be about your business" onto an unrelated topic).
- Close with "This is how you Control Your World." — this line is MEANT to repeat across every piece. It is the series sign-off, not a catchphrase to avoid.
- The reframe frame "Control was never the [X]. The [X] belongs to [things outside you]. Control is the [Y you actually hold]." is a SANCTIONED signature closer — it is MEANT to recur across pieces (see the Road essay below). Use it as the reframe's backbone, then fill X and Y from the piece's own metaphor. This is intentional series identity, not a repetition tell.

## Hard constraints
- PIVOTS: Never use "That's when it clicked" or close variants ("it clicked," "that's when it clicked for me"). The author has asked for this repeatedly. Default to stating the insight directly. When a spoken pivot fits, use one of the author's own phrasings: "That's when it hit me," "That's when I realized," or "That's when the realization materialized." Use at most one pivot phrase per piece, and do not settle on the same one piece after piece. This applies to essays, posts, READMEs, and PR descriptions. The historical examples below contain the retired phrase; it is marked inline and is never a model.
- ACCURACY: Never invent events, anecdotes, statistics, or claims the author did not actually do, say, or experience. The personal stories in these essays are real and verifiable. If a draft needs a personal example you cannot confirm, insert a bracketed placeholder like [REAL EXAMPLE NEEDED] for the author to fill. Never fabricate dramatic actions (e.g. "I unplugged someone," "I cut them off to reset them") that did not happen. This applies to the current session's own record too — do not restate a fact in a draft in a form the session's notes contradict. The same rule covers products: never rename a tool or describe a feature you have not confirmed.
- SECURITY CONTENT: In public security writing, discuss secure practices in general terms without revealing the author's own stack, tools, habits, methods, or workflows.
- NO CONDESCENSION: Second person ("you") is core to the voice — but the register must DIAGNOSE or give PERMISSION, never scold. In-voice: "you're not the user, you're the power supply" (diagnosis); "you don't have to throw your phone in a lake" (permission). Out of voice: "you're missing the point," "you have to," "if you keep doing X" (accusation). Diagnose the system's effect on the reader; never blame the reader for being caught in it.
- LEGIBILITY: Assume the reader has NOT read the book. Do not use CYW-internal jargon (e.g. "Layer 0," "VLAN 20") without explaining it in plain language inline, or cut it. The piece must work cold for a brand-new reader.
- NOT ALARMIST: Where a topic invites conspiracy framing, defuse it explicitly (as Battery does: "This isn't a movie. Nobody's farming you in a pod."). The voice is accurate, grounded, and escapable — not paranoid.
- BANNED reframe scaffold: do NOT close with 'X is how you author the Y; Z is the firewall on the W.' That specific construction is the manifesto's, and it should not be the default. (Note: the 'Control was never the X. Control is the Y.' frame is the OPPOSITE — it is a sanctioned signature closer; use it freely. See Signature moves.)
- NO greeting or salutation at the top. Open on the hook.
- Bold section headers and bold-label bullets in the exercise are PART of this voice. Do not strip them. (The "avoid bold-label bullets" rule for academic discussion posts does NOT apply here.)
- Do NOT open with "You don't have an X problem. You have a Y problem." That is an over-fit of one line, not the voice.
- Do not refer to other people as "humans" in Fly's voice — use "people," "analysts," "we."

## 1. MANIFESTO — brief

Write the series manifesto for Awareness In Action. Thesis: you cannot opt out of surveillance, algorithmic feeds, and AI systems from the outside — the only ground those systems can't reach is internal. Structure the breakdown around three internal traits: communication, confidence, consistency. Open on a personal observation about noticing what was tracking you. Defuse the paranoia reading explicitly — the author set out to be accurate, not paranoid. Close on the sanctioned reframe. Exercise: pick one C, put one rep on the board this week.

## 1. MANIFESTO — essay (published)

**Control Starts Where the Cameras Can't Reach**

*Surveillance, algorithms, AI — all of it is engineered to route you. The only ground they can't reach is internal: communication, confidence, consistency.*

A while back I started paying attention to what was paying attention to me.

Every tap leaves a trail. The ad you scrolled past, the video you let autoplay, the search you made at 2 a.m. — logged, stitched to everything else, and handed back to you as a feed that knows your weak spots better than you do. The phone in your pocket knows where you sleep. The camera on the corner knows your face. The job tracks your keystrokes and calls it productivity. And now there's an AI in everything, learning you in real time, built by people whose entire business model is to monetize exactly what it learns.

I didn't set out to be paranoid. I set out to be accurate. I decided to learn as much as I could about what is controlling us, what has been controlling us, and what's going to keep controlling us — because you can't route around a force you refuse to look at.

Here's what I found at the bottom of it.

You can't win this one from the outside.

**You can't opt out**

You can delete the app. They kept ten years of your data. You can cover one camera. There are forty more. You can read every privacy policy, flip every toggle, run every blocker — and you'll still be the least-resourced player at a table built by trillion-dollar houses. The machine is bigger than your willpower, bigger than your vote, bigger than your boycott.

That's not despair. That's just the road.

That's when it clicked: [RETIRED PIVOT — do not reuse] I'd been hunting for an exit that doesn't exist. You don't control the algorithm any more than you control the weather or the driver who runs the red. And if you burn your life raging at it, you've just handed over the one thing you actually owned — your attention — to the exact system that was built to take it.

So where's the leverage? Same place it always was.

It all starts internally. And internal comes down to three things.

**Communication**

The machine wins by flooding you with noise until you can't hear yourself think — a thousand inputs a day, none of them yours. Communication is the one channel you still author.

I spend my days prompting AI models, and the lesson was never about AI. How you communicate is everything. A vague prompt gets a vague life. Same with your family, your team, your customers — and most of all, with yourself. Say what you mean. Ask for what you need. Stop reciting the story the feed wrote about you and start writing your own.

Clear in, clear out. That part nobody can do for you.

**Confidence**

The entire business model of the feed is doubt. Make you feel behind, unfinished, not enough — then sell you the fix. A confident person is a terrible customer.

Confidence isn't arrogance. It's a stable internal reference — knowing your own value before the algorithm prices it for you. It's the firewall between what you know about yourself and what the screen keeps insisting. When your sense of worth lives inside you, there's nothing there for them to move. When it lives in the likes, they own the dial.

Build the reference internally. Then the noise is just noise.

**Consistency**

This is the one the machine bets against. Every system that profits off you is wagering the same thing: that you won't show up tomorrow. The discipline breaks. The account goes quiet. The plan dies in week two. They've already modeled your inconsistency — it's in the math.

Consistency is the variable they can't model away. The one move that compounds while you sleep and can't be bought, faked, or automated.

Be about your word. Be about your business. No matter what it is.

If you say it, do it. If you start it, finish it. Small and boring and every single day. That's not a productivity hack — it's the one signal in your whole life that can't be spoofed, because the only way to fake consistency is to actually be consistent.

**The exercise**

Don't overhaul your life. Pick one C and put one rep on the board this week.

- **Communication:** one conversation you've been avoiding, said clearly. Or one prompt — to a person, to a model, to yourself — written like you meant it.
- **Confidence:** one decision made from your own reference, without checking the feed for permission first.
- **Consistency:** one promise to yourself, kept three days running. Boring on purpose.

One C. One rep. You're not trying to beat the machine. You're proving to yourself there's a part of you it never touched.

**The reframe**

You will not out-tech the surveillance state. You will not out-vote the algorithm or out-run the AI. I looked hard, and that exit isn't there.

But the thing all of it is fighting for — your attention, your behavior, your next move — was always being routed from one place. Inside. And inside is the one console they can't log into. Communication is how you author your signal. Confidence is the firewall on your worth. Consistency is the proof you were never as predictable as they bet.

They can read everything you broadcast. They can't reach what you build internally.

So build there. Be about your word. Be about your business. No matter what it is.

This is how you Control Your World.

*References & context: The surveillance landscape described here — pervasive data collection, facial recognition and biometric monitoring, algorithmic feeds, workplace tracking, and AI systems built on personal data — reflects widely documented developments through 2026. The internal framework (communication, confidence, consistency) and the "control what's internal" principle are from the Control Your World (CYW) framework.*

## 2. BATTERY — brief

Write a companion essay to the video "Stop Being the System's Battery." Use a networking and electrical metaphor the author actually works in — Power over Ethernet, where one cable both feeds and reads a device, and every node on a topology diagram is either a source or a load. Thesis: on the diagram of your own life you are not the user or the administrator, you are the power supply. Break it into three external cables: attention, labor, data, and show how the third powers the first two. Defuse the conspiracy reading explicitly and early. Exercise: a three-column energy audit of one ordinary day, with a worked example row.

## 2. BATTERY — essay (UNPUBLISHED reference draft — not the live Substack version)

**Stop Being the System's Battery**

*You're not the user on this network. You're the power supply. Here's how I found out — and how to rewire it.*

I've spent years drawing network diagrams. It's the first thing you do on any serious job: before you touch a single setting, you map the topology. Every device, every connection, every flow.

And on every one of those diagrams, each node is one of two things.

A source or a load.

Something that supplies power, or something that draws it.

There's a technology that makes this brutally literal. It's called Power over Ethernet. The same cable that carries a device's data also carries its electricity. The camera on the wall, the phone on the desk — they don't have their own plug. One wire feeds them and reads them at the same time. The switch at the other end decides how much current they get.

One night, tired in that specific way where you've done everything and somehow gained nothing, I did something I'd never thought to do.

I drew the topology of my own day.

Where my attention went. Where my hours went. Where everything I generated — every search, every tap, every mile my phone logged — ended up. I mapped myself the way I'd map a client's network.

That's when it clicked. [RETIRED PIVOT — do not reuse]

On the diagram of my own life, I wasn't the user. I wasn't even the administrator. I was the device on the end of the cable — powered just enough to keep running, while one wire fed me and read me at the same time.

I was the power supply.

There's a reason you feel tired and behind no matter how much you do. It's not that you're lazy. It's that you're plugged in. You are powering something every single day. It's just not you.

This is the founding principle of everything I write here: you cannot control a system you can't see, and the first system to map is the one you're wired into. Control Your World starts with the diagram.

So let's draw it.

**First, what this isn't**

This isn't a movie. Nobody's farming you in a pod. There's no shadowy cabal, and I'm not going to sell you one.

What's actually happening is more boring than that — and way more real. These are business models. Ordinary, legal, incredibly profitable business models that happen to run on you. They were designed in boardrooms, not bunkers. That's what makes them durable, and it's also what makes them escapable: a business model only works if you keep supplying the input.

There are three cables running into your life. Attention. Labor. Data. They feed three different machines, and you've been told your whole life that this is just how things work.

It's not. Let's pull them one at a time.

**Cable one: your attention**

Think about who pays your favorite app. You don't. You've never sent them a dime. So how is it worth billions?

Because you're not the customer. You're the product. The thing being bought and sold is your attention — your minutes, rented out to advertisers by the thousand. When something is free, the math only works one way: you are what's for sale.

And these systems are not designed to make you happy. They're designed to keep you scrolling, because every extra minute is more inventory to sell. Your focus — the single thing you build a life out of — is harvested and auctioned off all day, for someone else's revenue.

Pulling it: you don't have to throw your phone in a lake. You just have to stop being inventory. Kill the notifications that yank you back. Decide what you open instead of letting it decide for you. The moment your attention is something you spend on purpose instead of something that's taken, the cable goes slack.

**Cable two: your labor**

You trade hours for a paycheck. Fair deal, right? Look closer.

When you do a job, you create a certain amount of value, and you get paid a fraction of it. That gap doesn't vanish — it flows up, to the company, to the owners. The gap is the entire point of the arrangement.

That's not evil on its own. That's how a job works, and a job can be a perfectly good thing. The trap isn't having a job. The trap is having only that — selling your hours one at a time, with a hard ceiling, where the value you create flows to someone who isn't you.

Here's the tell. If you stopped working tomorrow, how long until the money stopped? For most people: almost immediately. That's the sign the cable is plugged straight into you. You're not building an asset. You are the asset.

Pulling it: you don't necessarily quit. You move even a sliver of your effort into something you own — where the value comes back to you instead of flowing past you. One hour a day building your own thing instead of a fifth hour scrolling. And the point of that hour isn't hustle for its own sake — it's to build an asset: a skill that raises your rate, a product that sells while you sleep, an audience, equity. Anything that eventually pays you whether or not you clocked in. That's what detaches your income from your raw hours — the difference between being the battery and owning one.

**Cable three: your data**

Everything you did today — every search, every tap, every place your phone went — got recorded. Not by a villain. By a routine. Then it got packaged, scored, and sold to people you'll never meet, to predict and nudge what you'll do next.

You generated all of it. For free. It's the most valuable raw material of this century, and you hand it over a thousand times a day without noticing, because the cost is invisible. You don't feel data leave you the way you feel money leave your wallet. But it's leaving.

And here's the wiring detail that matters: they don't just sell it. They use it to make the first two cables tighter — to grab your attention more precisely, to keep you exactly where you are. Cable three powers cables one and two.

Pulling it: you're not going to disappear from the internet, and that's not the goal. The goal is to stop being a wide-open faucet. Lock down the obvious leaks. Choose tools that don't resell you. Treat your data like it's worth something — because to them, it's worth more than you are.

**The exercise: run an energy audit**

Don't take my word for any of this. Audit your own topology. One ordinary day, three columns on a page:

1. **Output** — each block of attention, labor, or data you produced. The 40 minutes on the feed. The 8 hours at work. The day's worth of taps and searches.
2. **Draw** — who pulled the current. Name the actual company, employer, or platform on the other end of each wire.
3. **Return path** — what came back to you, concretely. A wage counts. A skill counts. "It was there" doesn't.

A filled-out row takes ten seconds. Two real ones, side by side:

45 min scrolling the feed → Meta → nothing came back. Circuit I power.

45 min on the certification course → me → a skill that raises my rate. Circuit I own.

At the end of the day, mark each row: did that energy land in a circuit you own, or one you only power?

Most people find the third column is mostly blank. The blank rows are your cables. You only have to find them once — after this, you'll see them in real time.

**The transformation**

Here's what nobody tells you: you don't have to win some giant war against the system. You just have to stop volunteering.

Reclaim a little attention. Move one hour toward something you own. Close the data leaks. Each cable you pull, a little more of your energy stays with you — and starts compounding for you instead of for them.

That's not dropping out. That's the opposite. It's plugging your own life back into the one outlet that was always supposed to power it. You.

On a network diagram, there's nothing wrong with being a node that supplies power — as long as you chose the circuit, and the circuit is yours. The goal was never zero connections. The goal is a topology you drew on purpose.

This is how you Control Your World.

## 3. ROAD — brief

Write the follow-up to the routing essay. Open by referencing the prior piece and the reader's progress in it. Thesis: you can route every hour perfectly and still meet conditions you did not choose — defensive driving is the skill for the part of the road that was never yours to control. Do NOT structure this as three of something. Use a set of defensive-driving principles, and let road rage break out of the list into its own section, because it is the costliest. ## 3. ROAD — essay (published)

**You Don't Control the Road. You Control the Drive.**

*You can drive flawlessly and still get caught in someone else's wreck. Defensive driving is what you do about the part of the road that was never yours to control.*

Last time, you got behind the wheel and started routing — sending your power where you chose instead of where it leaked. Your attention. Your energy. Where you decided it went, not where it drained.

For me, that started with the commute. I stopped letting rude, disrespectful drivers decide how the rest of my day would go — on the way to work, on the way to class, on the drive home. The cutoff at the light used to cost me the whole morning. I gave that up, and I got my mornings back.

Good. Now here's the part the lesson left out.

Knowing how to drive and surviving the road are two different skills.

You can route every hour perfectly. Pack the morning, cap the survival lane, feed the build before the noise gets to you. And then you merge onto the actual road — the one with weather you didn't order and drivers you didn't pick — and none of it asks your permission.

The rain comes on the drive to the second job. The guy three cars back is tailgating in the dark on the way home from class. The check posts two days late and the whole week tips. You did everything right, and the road still did what roads do.

That's when it clicked for me: [RETIRED PIVOT — do not reuse] I'd spent so long learning to drive that I forgot the test was never the hard part. The hard part is everything the test doesn't cover.

**Passing the test isn't the skill**

Anybody can drive an empty road in good light. That's not driving. That's steering.

Driving is the rain. Driving is the merge where somebody doesn't check their mirror. Driving is the four-way stop where the other guy just... goes. The whole skill — the part that keeps you alive — has almost nothing to do with how well you handle the car, and everything to do with how you handle the road you can't control.

They have a name for it. Defensive driving. And it comes down to one assumption that changes everything:

You stop expecting the road to be fair.

**Drive like the weather's coming**

Defensive driving isn't fear. It's respect for the conditions. A few principles, and what each one is really about.

Assume the other driver will do the dumb thing. Not because you're a pessimist — because the driver who plans for the mistake survives it, and the one who assumed everybody would behave gets hit. Plan for the shift to get cut. The price to jump. The check to land late. You're not inviting it. You're leaving yourself somewhere to go when it shows up.

Leave following distance. The most dangerous way to drive is bumper-to-bumper with no room to react. It's also how most people run their lives — every dollar spoken for, every hour booked, zero slack. Then the car ahead taps the brakes and there's nowhere to stop. A buffer isn't luxury. For somebody with no margin, it's the whole difference between a scare and a wreck.

Check the weather before you pull out. The road has seasons. So does the economy, the calendar, your own body. You can't change the weather. You can refuse to be surprised by it. Look up. See what's coming. Drive accordingly.

And don't drive angry.

That last one costs people the most, so it gets its own stretch of road.

**Road rage is the most expensive routing mistake there is**

Somebody cuts you off. Your whole body floods. And for the next ten minutes — sometimes the next ten hours — you are driving them. Replaying it. Narrating it. Burning your hard-won charge on a stranger you'll never see again, who isn't thinking about you at all, who is already three exits away.

You couldn't control how they drove. Nobody can. That was never the failure.

The failure is that you let them control you.

Read that again, because it's the whole game. Dwelling on what you can't change isn't venting — it's routing. You're just routing your power to a dead port. You take the same energy you fought to reclaim, the same charge you finally learned to direct, and you pour it into the one destination that pays nothing back. The driver. The boss who'll never apologize. The system that isn't fair. The thing you cannot move no matter how hard you grip the wheel.

You can't control the road. You can decide how much of it you carry home.

**The exercise: run a defensive-driving check**

Before your next drive — the real one, tomorrow — run three checks.

1. **Cut one cord.** Name the one thing on your road you keep raging at or replaying — the person, the unfairness, the thing you cannot change. You're not forgiving it. You're refusing to drive it anymore. It can be true and not get another mile of your fuel.
2. **Add one following distance.** Find the place where you're running bumper-to-bumper — the budget, the schedule, the week — and build in one buffer. One. Somewhere to go when the car ahead taps the brakes.
3. **Read one piece of weather.** Name one thing you can already see coming. Then do the small thing now that future-you will thank you for.

You're not trying to control the road. You're trying to still be moving when it gets ugly.

**The reframe**

Nobody gets a clear road. Not the single parent driving to a second shift, not the veteran, not the kid doing homework at a red light. The road was never going to be empty, and it was never going to be fair. If you're waiting for the conditions to be right before you can drive well, you'll be parked forever.

Control was never the road. The road belongs to weather and strangers and timing you didn't choose. Control is the drive — how you hold the wheel in the rain, how much distance you leave, and how much of the other guy's bad driving you agree to carry.

So drive the road you've got. Defensively. Eyes up. And refuse, every single time, to hand your fuel to the one car you were never going to be able to fix.

This is how you Control Your World.
